What this bucket is

Model shoots are the most common paid deliverable in commercial photography — a brand, agency, or magazine commissions a set of images of a person (founder, model, creator, talent) wearing the brand's clothes, holding the brand's product, or just being the brand's face. Everything in this bucket has the same subject type: a human you have to keep identifiably the same across every shot.

That's the bucket's signature constraint. Get identity wrong and the deliverable falls apart — the magazine can tell, the client can tell, the audience can tell. Everything else is a slot you can swap.

The Model-Shoot lock / variable map

Same universal pattern from the meta-page — but here are the columns for THIS bucket:

Always LOCKED for a model shootAlways VARIABLE per shot
Identity — face, body, hair (via character sheet)
Color grade — film stock or LUT-less spec
Lens family — 35/50/85/100, kept consistent
Anti-keywords — no AI tells, no anime eyes, no Pinterest filter 		Setting — studio / cafe / street / home / outdoor
Lighting direction + quality — soft window / hard sun / studio key
Pose & composition — three-quarter / full body / detail
Wardrobe (sometimes locked, often varied per look)

If you don't lock the identity, the set falls apart. If you don't vary the setting and light, the set looks like duplicates of one shot. Both rules at once = a real shoot.

If…Then…

F.M.01 — Editorial Portrait — brand founder series

Use when: the deliverable is a founder / creator / exec portrait set for an About page, a press feature, an editorial profile. Warm, restrained, on-brand, six shots covering studio + work + life.

Theme

A magazine-grade editorial portrait series. Warm earth tones, Kodak Portra grade, soft natural light wherever possible, candid documentary energy. The subject is the same person across every shot; what changes is where she is, what she's doing, and how the light lands.

Setup recipe — the locked half

Paste this once per shoot. Attach the subject's character sheet as the reference image. Then for each shot below, swap the setting, lighting, and pose description, leave everything else.

{

  "subject": "the woman in the reference image, wearing <SLOT — wardrobe>",
  "setting": "<SLOT>",
  "lighting": "<SLOT>",
  "camera_lens": "Sony A7R V, 50mm f/2.0, sharp focus on the eyes, shallow depth of field",

}

Results — the actual generated set

Same character sheet on every shot. Same setup recipe. Eight rows = eight images that read like one shoot. The three green-highlighted cafe rows above land as three legitimate frames of the same moment — the kind of triplet a working photographer would ship together.

Eight rows in the shot table, eight paste-and-generate calls, one coherent shoot. The cafe trio shows what locking actually buys you: same world, three legitimate angles.

Watch the trio. Sweater identical. Window identical. Light direction identical. Mug identical. Only the camera moved. That's how you know the locked half held.

Now ship it

Swap the character reference (replace this woman with your client's founder). Re-run the same rows. You have a different founder's shoot in one render session. Then pick one or two of your scenes and add their 2b / 2c angles — your six-shot shoot becomes a nine-shot shoot for the cost of two extra generations.

F.M.02 — Lifestyle / UGC — creator + product

Use when: the brief is paid social, creator content, or any "feels-like-a-real-customer" deliverable. iPhone aesthetic, slightly imperfect framing, product naturally in frame across day-in-the-life moments.

↓ Sheet built via F-Sheet (Recipe F.SHEET.01) — attached as reference on every shot below:

Theme

A wellness / skincare creator's day with the product — six shots from morning unboxing to on-the-go. Reads as filmed on a phone, not on a studio rig. The product appears in every shot but isn't the hero of every shot — it's part of his life.

The realism lock — read this before you generate. UGC fails the second it looks polished. The most important keys below are the anti-polish ones: visible skin texture, pores, micro-imperfections, NO beauty filter, NO skin smoothing, NO DSLR look. Without those, every model defaults to studio-polish and the shoot reads as an ad pretending to be UGC. With them, the same person looks real.

Setup recipe — the locked half

{
  "image_type": "iPhone candid photograph, NOT a professional shoot",
  "subject": "the man in the reference image, <SLOT — wardrobe>",
  "product": "a clean amber-glass dropper bottle, ~80ml, minimal cream label",
  "setting": "<SLOT>",
  "lighting": "<SLOT — always natural, often harsh/unbalanced>",
  "camera_lens": "iPhone 15 Pro main lens, hand-held, slight tilt, candid framing",
  "color_grade": "natural with slight uneven warm/cool cast, true-to-life skin tones",
  "skin_texture": "visible pores, micro-asymmetry, slight redness, light stubble, NOT retouched",
  "no_beauty_filter": true,
  "no_skin_smoothing": true,
  "no_studio_polish": true,
  "no_dslr_look": true,
  "no_commercial_perfection": true,
  "identity_lock_strength": "high",
  "character_reference": "<character sheet attached>"
}

Results

Look at the mirror shot: fingerprint smudges, slight redness around the nose, no glamour. The unboxing shot: visible arm hair, casual tilt to the framing, off-center. That's what the anti-polish lock buys — a person, not a model.

Now ship it

Swap the product to your client's SKU. Keep the character. Swap one or two settings for the brand's actual world. The anti-polish locks are doing the heavy lifting — students often try to "improve" these by removing the imperfections, and accidentally re-make an ad. Resist. Keep the skin-texture key.

F.M.03 — Fashion Editorial — magazine drop

Use when: the brief is a fashion brand's editorial campaign, a magazine spread, a cover-style image. One model, multiple looks, deliberately polarizing poses — angular, expressive, dropped-shoulder, head-tilted-away. The clothes are the variable; the model + the editorial intensity are the constants.

Project start — input → sheet

The agency gave us one test shot of the model. We turned it into a 5-view character sheet via F-Sheet Recipe F.SHEET.01. That sheet locks the model's identity across every editorial look — so wardrobe is the variable but the face holds across the whole spread.

↓ Sheet built via F-Sheet (Recipe F.SHEET.01) — attached as reference on every shot below:

Why this looks different from the UGC entry. Editorial fashion is not approachable. It is the opposite of approachable — it is angular, deliberate, sometimes uncomfortable. A lookbook that gives you "model standing with hands in pockets" reads as a DTC catalog. A lookbook that gives you a dropped shoulder, a twisted torso, a head tilted at a sharp angle reads as a magazine. That is the editorial choice, and that choice has to be IN your prompt.

Theme

Helmut Lang / The Row / Rick Owens editorial energy. Hard sculptural side light, deep editorial contrast, angular and confident posing. The model is detached and graphic, not warm and friendly. Skin texture stays visible — modern editorial embraces the real face.

Setup recipe — the locked half

{
  "image_type": "editorial fashion magazine photograph",
  "subject": "the woman in the reference image, wearing <SLOT — outfit>",
  "setting": "<SLOT — clean studio cyc, color noted>",
  "lighting": "single dramatic hard side light from camera-right, deep editorial contrast, sculptural shadow",
  "camera_lens": "Hasselblad H6D, 80mm f/4, sharp throughout, editorial-aggressive framing",
  "color_grade": "cool neutral editorial, deep blacks, faithful skin tones, restrained",
  "pose_register": "angular, deliberate, dropped shoulder, twisted torso, head tilted, gaze detached",
  "no_catalog_pose": true,
  "no_hands_in_pockets": true,
  "no_pinterest_filter": true,
  "no_anime_polish": true,
  "no_dtc_blandness": true,
  "identity_lock_strength": "high",
  "character_reference": "<character sheet attached>"
}

Shot table — the variable half (5 shots)

#SettingOutfit (the lookbook's variable)Pose / composition
1Bone-white cycOversized cream chunky knit + wide cream trousersWeight dramatically on one hip, shoulder dropped, head tilted away, gaze down
2Dark-gray gradient cycBlack single-breasted suit, no shirt under, deep VHand at back of neck, elbow up, head tilted down, eyes up at camera
3Bone-white cycSilk slip dress under vintage leather jacket falling off one shoulderLeaning back, weight on heel, wrist twisted, bored-cool expression
4Warm-gray painted backdropIndigo denim + crisp white tee tucked inCaught mid-turn, looking back over shoulder, hard shadow on backdrop
5Pure black seamlessThin black turtleneck (close-up)Head tilted sharply, intense gaze, hand grazing under jaw, cover crop

Results

One model. Five looks. Light + lens + grade locked. Pose register locked at "editorial angular." The clothes change; the editorial intensity does not.

The pose-register lock is the secret here. If you only lock light + grade + lens, every shot drifts toward catalog poise. Adding pose_register as a locked key — "angular, dropped shoulder, twisted torso, head tilted, gaze detached" — forces the model into editorial territory on every row, even when the only thing changing is wardrobe.

Now ship it

Replace the five outfits with your client's hero pieces. Keep the editorial recipe. Generate. The shoot reads as a magazine spread, not a catalog. If the client wants a softer version, weaken the pose register one notch (drop "dropped shoulder," keep "head tilted, gaze detached"). If they want more confrontation, push the lighting harder (add "single hard rim light, deep noir contrast").
